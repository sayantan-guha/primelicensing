Adaptation Notes
Karagar is a historical mystery built on an impossible discovery: a routine headcount at a colonial-era prison turns up one prisoner too many, in a cell sealed for decades, a man who is mute, ageless by his own account, and impossible to disprove. An interpreter brought in to translate his signs believes she is there to expose a fraud. She becomes the unwitting key to a reckoning that reaches back generations.
Telugu adaptation, Karagaram / Bandikhana: Set inside a British-era Rajahmundry jail days from demolition, the prisoner claims to have fought beside freedom fighter Alluri Sitarama Raju and killed the officer who betrayed him, writing his testimony in an archaic Grandhika Telugu script. Mahati, the interpreter searching for her own missing father, eventually uncovers the deeper truth: he is David Adams, a war-child born of a fictionalised regional massacre (inspired by the 1985 Karamchedu massacre), and his freedom-fighter myth was built only to draw one man, Rudra Pratap Ramaswamy, close enough to kill. A parallel thread, a jailer hiding his own son under a false identity, escalates the back half into a full prison uprising.
Hindi adaptation, Karagar: The same discovery relocates to Sahiwal Central Jail in Pakistani Punjab, January 1991, in a cell sealed since Partition. Here the prisoner claims to have killed Robert Clive, the man who opened Hindustan to the East India Company, and is worshipped by the other inmates as Gazi Baba. Mehr, the interpreter, is secretly pregnant and hunting her own vanished father, pulled into a wound that traces back to the Rawalpindi killings of 1947. The reveal strips the myth to something rawer: David Adam, a Partition-born son avenging not two centuries of grievance but one mother's unspoken trauma, using the jail as the last place he can bring her attacker within reach.
Both versions root an ageless, impossible mystery in a real, buried history a state would rather not reopen, whether that is the caste-driven massacres of 1980s Andhra or the mass sexual violence of Partition. The prison setting, sealed cells, unregistered prisoners, jailers protecting their own, gives the show a closed, paranoid world where institutional silence is as dangerous as the killer at its centre.
Karagar already has a second season written. Years after the first season's reckoning, the men who helped bury the massacre start turning up dead, and suspicion falls on David again, until he reveals two rival forces are now in play, a militant orphaned by the same massacre plotting to turn the survivors' deaths into a televised uprising, and the dead tormentor's own estranged son racing to erase every remaining witness. The format is built to keep expanding the same buried history outward, not to close it.

Adaptation Notes
Roktofolok is a tantric horror thriller built on a thousand-year-old evil that now hunts through social media. The show begins with young women vanishing in modern Bengal, lured online by a charming predator whose victims share a pattern only one man recognises. That man is Krishnananda Agambagish, a reclusive tantric scholar whose knowledge of the occult is personal not academic. Visions come to him uninvited and precise, and his deepest suspicion is that he is not investigating the cycle, he is already inside it. The pattern traces back a thousand years to Sompura Mahavihar, a Buddhist monastery where a tantric sect performed blood rituals to a bloodthirsty entity called Bajraketu. The sect's relic, the Roktofolok, carries his curse.
Now Bajraketu has reincarnated in a charming professional named Tenia, who grooms young women through a fake online persona called Sam. The show's sharpest insight is that what social media does to vulnerable young women today is what this evil has always done, just faster and at scale.
The mythology sits inside Vajrayana, the tantric branch of Mahayana Buddhism that flourished in the Pala dynasty's great monasteries, and its grammar of tantric ritual is recognised across both Hindu and Buddhist tantric traditions in India. Bihar is the natural Hindi home, where Nalanda is the most recognised Buddhist mahavihara in pan-Indian memory, and Vikramshila nearby, built by the same Pala king who built Sompura, gives the Vajrayana tantric lineage an exact match.
Online grooming of young women is not a horror invention. Fake personas, slow coercion, and physical harm are now documented across Indian cities and conversations. Roktofolok places this current crisis inside a thousand-year-old mythology and shows that the evil is not new.
The first cycle holds one season, and each season opens a new Roktofolok because Krishnananda is part of the cycle. The adaptation must keep his work intellectual and ritual. He investigates, deduces, and breaks the curse through occult practice. His power is sharper than physical force, and the audience who watch him, outthink an evil older than him.

Parnashavarir Shaap is a folk-horror mystery built around Nirendranath Bhaduri, the veteran occult specialist, addressed in the show as Bhaduri Moshai. The first case finds him on a hillside, where four urban friends on vacation have accidentally disturbed an ancient relic in a sacred forest. Adaptation Notes
Dainee is a survival thriller built around two half-sisters and the mother who once forced them to face everything together. Pata returns to Jharkhand after years abroad, drawn back only by her father's death and the inheritance she is counting on to save her from a failing life overseas. The one condition is that her estranged half-sister Lata must also be present to claim it. Pata finds her just as a village mob is about to burn her alive, having declared her a dainee, a witch.
Pata steals Lata from the stake and the two flee to a rural hospital, dragging the entire village's fury behind them. What follows is a siege: hundreds outside demanding the witch be handed over, a handful of doctors, patients, and the two sisters trapped inside. Behind the mob stands Bhim, a local strongman whose word carries more weight than the police, and whose real motive for branding Lata a witch is a rejection he never forgave.
Witch-hunting is not an invented horror. The film sits inside that real, ongoing crisis rather than inventing one.
The setting is deliberately remote, deep in Palamu's forests where roads narrow into dirt tracks and mobile signal disappears, a landscape that isolates the siege completely and strips away any easy rescue. It is a story already built for a Hindi, pan-Indian audience, not adapted into one.
Designed as a complete, self-contained theatrical film, Dainee closes its central story in full, but the world it opens, of strongmen who run villages by fear and the women left to survive them, carries potential to extend into future films in the same universe. At its centre is a mother's old instruction finally honoured: that the two sisters would have to look after each other.

Dainee is a survival thriller built around two half-sisters and the mother who once forced them to face everything together. Pata returns to Jharkhand after years abroad, drawn back only by her father's death and the inheritance she is counting on to save her from a failing life overseas. The one condition is that her estranged half-sister Lata must also be present to claim it. Pata finds her just as a village mob is about to burn her alive, having declared her a dainee, a witch.
Pata steals Lata from the stake and the two flee to a rural hospital, dragging the entire village's fury behind them. What follows is a siege: hundreds outside demanding the witch be handed over, a handful of doctors, patients, and the two sisters trapped inside. Behind the mob stands Bhim, a local strongman whose word carries more weight than the police, and whose real motive for branding Lata a witch is a rejection he never forgave.
Witch-hunting is not an invented horror. Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and the wider tribal belt still record dozens of dainee killings every year, usually against women who are widowed, unmarried, or simply inconvenient, and usually blamed for unrelated tragedy, in this case an encephalitis outbreak whose real culprit hides behind the accusation. The film sits inside that real, ongoing crisis rather than inventing one.
The setting is deliberately remote, deep in Palamu's forests where roads narrow into dirt tracks and mobile signal disappears, a landscape that isolates the siege completely and strips away any easy rescue. It is a story already built for a Hindi, pan-Indian audience, not adapted into one.
Designed as a complete, self-contained theatrical film, Dainee closes its central story in full, but the world it opens, of strongmen who run villages by fear and the women left to survive them, carries potential to extend into future films in the same universe. At its centre is a mother's old instruction finally honoured: that the two sisters would have to look after each other.

The Opportunity
Three seasons in. More emotionally grounded than any legal show in its weight class. A feature film already in the franchise roadmap.
01
A specific kind of legal protagonist that does not exist yet
Legal drama has sharp-tongued virtuosos. What it does not have enough of is the morally earnest underdog who wins through empathy rather than genius. Achinta Aich is built entirely around that quality and three seasons of Bengali audiences have found it irresistible.
02
The character is the unfair advantage
Achinta wins cases not through legal genius but through stubborn decency. The Bengali audience's attachment to him is to a specific human quality. That quality translates entirely across language and setting.
03
Acquisition comes with a slate, not a pilot
Season 1 available. Season 2 available. Season 3 in production. Feature film planned. This is not a format pitch with proof of concept pending. It is a franchise with three years of audience validation already behind it.

Adaptation Notes
Hindi adaptation, Advocate Achyut Acharya: He is a Kandivali notary running a practice of society NOCs and lost tiffin boxes, a life built small enough to never fail at. He lives with his widowed father Hasmukhbhai, who has spent thirty years calling him nalayak, good-for-nothing, and his only real ally is Sunny Tripathi, a failed actor immune to anyone's approval, which is exactly why he can spot when Achyut's calm is a performance.
In the season's first case, the Marfatia case, Achyut defends a wife framed for her husband's murder against a legendary opposing lawyer, taking it for one reason he cannot refuse: his father, for the first time in thirty years, tells him to fight, and it sounds like belief. The trial uncovers a film dynasty's buried secret, that the victim was never their bloodline at all, and Achyut walks out having done the one thing no senior advocate could: he listened.
The second case opens on the morning of Achyut's own engagement, interrupted by a city baying for an execution. He pulls a driver back from the gallows hours before he is due to hang for a murder the whole country has already decided he committed, and uncovers a wealthy family's cover-up for their own son's crime. This time Hasmukhbhai stands guard beside him rather than passing judgment, and Devanshi Mehta, his partner by now rather than just a suitor, fights the case alongside him.
Both cases are built on the same asymmetry: a courtroom, a newsroom, and a city instinctively agreeing on who deserves to be believed and who does not, whether that is a grieving wife assumed guilty or a poor driver assumed guilty before any evidence is heard. Achyut wins neither by outarguing anyone, but by outlistening a world that stopped listening long ago.
Achyut's arc runs across two self-contained cases, a man who first has to learn he is worth defending, then spends that belief defending people the world has already written off, a structure built to open a new case, and a new verdict the city has already wrongly decided, every season.

The Opportunity
A senior police officer's daughter vanishes after a party. The officer is a mother. She cannot file the case herself. She investigates anyway.
01
The premise has one degree of separation from unbearable
Nikhoj works because it is never really about the investigation. It is about a woman using her professional skills to survive the worst thing that can happen to a parent. Bengali audiences watched two full seasons because they could not stop. That is not genre loyalty. That is emotional hostage-taking. It adapts.
02
Two seasons that function as one complete story
Both seasons together form a single continuous narrative arc. A finished, bingeable story with no loose ends and no development exposure. The emotional arc is complete. The mystery resolves. The audience gets closure.
03
The investigator-as-mother inversion
Every missing-child procedural puts the investigator at a professional distance from the case. Nikhoj eliminates that distance entirely. The person most qualified to find the child is the person least able to be objective. That inversion and the impossible tension it creates is the format's entire engine.

Franchise Potential: Both existing seasons function as one complete mystery arc, with the case-of-the-season format built to continue across future installments.
Adaptation Notes
Nikhoj is a crime thriller about a disgraced anchor accused of killing the intern who vanished with him, investigated by an officer secretly hunting for her own missing daughter, who turns out to be the missing girl herself. The anchor, the face his network sells as the conscience of the nation, wakes at dawn beside his own bloodstained car with no memory of the night before, and the officer assigned to break him is secretly the missing intern's estranged mother.
The Hindi version, Khoj, plays out across Delhi, with anchor Karan Mehra and ACP Kaveri Thakur: a media firestorm run by a rival channel, a family that disintegrates in public, and a homeless witness silenced before he can talk. The trail runs through Old Delhi's lanes to a mechanic, a lawyer, and a mercenary, before closing on the one man never under suspicion, the husband of the friend the original drug was meant for.
The Telugu version, Anveshana, plays out the same case beat for beat across Hyderabad, with anchor Aravind Varma and ACP Bhairavi, from Necklace Road to the Old City, right down to the mercenary caught alone aboard a moving MMTS train. The parallel adaptation shows the format traveling intact across two markets without losing any of its mechanics.
Nikhoj runs on trial-by-media as much as it runs on the murder itself: a city convicts a man in the press before any court does, and the investigation only survives because one officer refuses to accept the story everyone else has already agreed on. Underneath the procedural is a mother-daughter estrangement healed too late, and a small, overlooked complaint, a missing scooter dismissed as trivial, that turns out to hold the entire case together.
Built as a closed, twist-driven case, Nikhoj still ends with its investigator standing, changed but in place, structured so a new season can open a new case for the same officer, in either language, without needing to revisit this one.
